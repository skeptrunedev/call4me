**The short answer:** a **full cord** of firewood is 128 cubic feet of wood stacked tight, the size of a pile 4 feet high, 4 feet deep and 8 feet long. A **face cord** (also sold as a "rick" or a "fireplace cord") is one row of that pile: 4 feet high and 8 feet long, but only as deep as one piece of wood is long. So face cord vs full cord comes down to piece length. **At the common 16-inch length, 3 face cords make a full cord**, and one face cord is 42.7 cubic feet. With 12-inch pieces it takes 4, with 18-inch pieces about 2.7, and with 24-inch pieces 2.

"Face cord" has no legal size in any of the three states we checked. California follows the national model rule, which defines a cord as 128 cubic feet and bars "face cord" in firewood sales. Massachusetts goes further: firewood has to be advertised and sold in cubic feet, and even the word "cord" is barred. Minnesota lets a buyer and seller agree to anything, but its Commerce Department says face cord and rick have no legal definition, and its statute counts a stacked cord of split wood as 120 cubic feet.

To see what people actually get, we went back through the 48 firewood businesses we documented for our [Truckee](/blog/firewood-delivery-truckee), [Wellesley](/blog/firewood-delivery-wellesley-ma) and [Wayzata and Orono](/blog/firewood-delivery-wayzata-orono) guides, and re-checked the 34 that name a unit online against their own pages on November 27, 2026. Half of them, 17, sell a cord or a fraction of one without saying how big it is. The ones that do give a size don't agree on what a face cord is: one sells a pallet of about 35 cubic feet as a face cord. For what all this costs, see [how much a cord of firewood is](/blog/how-much-is-a-cord-of-firewood).

## Face cord vs full cord at common piece lengths

A face cord is 4 feet high by 8 feet long, so its volume is 32 square feet times the piece length. A full cord is 4 x 4 x 8 feet, or 128 cubic feet, whatever the piece length.

| Piece length | One face cord (4 ft high, 8 ft long, one row) | Face cords in a full cord | Share of a full cord |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12 inches | 32 cubic feet | 4 | 25% |
| **16 inches** | **42.7 cubic feet** | **3** | **33%** |
| 18 inches | 48 cubic feet | 2.67 | 38% |
| 20 inches | 53.3 cubic feet | 2.4 | 42% |
| 24 inches | 64 cubic feet | 2 | 50% |

Sixteen inches is the length you'll hear most. The owner of BostonFirewood.com told our caller, "Sixteen inches is the industry standard," and of the sellers below that give a piece length, most cut to 16 inches or close to it, some to 16 to 18 or 16 to 20. If your wood is cut shorter, a "face cord" holds less: a row of 12-inch pieces is a quarter cord, not a third.

A full cord doesn't have to be one 4 x 4 x 8 block. Three rows of 16-inch wood, each 4 feet high and 8 feet long, is a cord. So are two rows of 16-inch wood 4 feet high and 12 feet long. Hopkinton, Massachusetts' weights and measures page puts it plainly: "Any combination of these measurements is fine as long as it measures 128 cubic feet." Jay's Tree Care in Medina also sells a "half face cord," a 4 x 4 foot row of 16-inch wood: 21.3 cubic feet, a sixth of a cord.

A cord is measured stacked |

### The national rule: NIST Handbook 130

The national model for firewood sales is the Uniform Regulation for the Method of Sale of Commodities in [NIST Handbook 130](https://www.nist.gov/document/2026-hb-130-section-iv-b), which states can adopt into their own law (California does, below). Its 2026 edition defines a cord as "the amount of wood that is contained in a space of 128 ft³ when the wood is ranked and well stowed," and "ranked and well stowed" as "pieces of wood are placed in a line or row, with individual pieces touching and parallel to each other, and stacked in a compact manner."

Firewood "shall be advertised, offered for sale, and sold only by measure, using the term 'cord' and fractional parts of a cord or the cubic meter," apart from small packages and pressed logs. Section 2.4.4 bars the rest: "The terms 'face cord,' 'rack,' 'pile,' 'truckload,' or terms of similar import shall not be used when advertising, offering for sale, or selling wood for use as fuel." The rule names "rack," not "rick," and adds "terms of similar import." The seller also owes you a delivery ticket or invoice with, among other things, "the quantity delivered" and the price.

### California: a cord or a fraction of a cord

California's regulation, [4 CCR § 4530](https://www.cdfa.ca.gov/dms/docs/publications/2024/FRM_Chapter_10_Sections_4500-4532.pdf), adopts the Handbook 130 firewood section (2019 edition) by reference. The state Division of Measurement Standards' [buyer page](https://www.cdfa.ca.gov/dms/programs/qc/firewood.html) says it more bluntly: "Wood can only be sold by the cord or by fractions of a cord." "A cord must equal 128 cubic feet." "If it doesn't equal 128 cubic feet, it is not a cord!" And: "A seller may not legitimately use terms such as 'truckload,' 'face cord,' 'rack,' or 'pile' because these terms have no legally defined meaning."

None of the seven Truckee-area sellers below uses "face cord." Six sell cords and fractions of cords (Arbor Care sells split wood and logs with no cord unit online), and only one of the six gives the cord's dimensions.

### Massachusetts: cubic feet, not cords

Massachusetts law, [Chapter 94, Section 298](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXV/Chapter94/Section298), says firewood (wood cut longer than 8 inches and shorter than 4 feet) must be "advertised, offered for sale and sold only in terms of cubic feet or cubic meters," which it defines as the "closely stacked" volume delivered to the buyer. The terms "cord," "face cord," "pile" and "truckload" "shall not be used in the advertising and sale of cordwood or firewood." [Section 299](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXV/Chapter94/Section299) requires a delivery ticket or invoice at delivery showing "the quantity delivered to the purchaser in terms of cubic feet or cubic meters," the date and the price.

Hopkinton's [Sealer of Weights and Measures](https://www.hopkintonma.gov/departments/land_use/sealer_of_weights___measures.php) page sums it up: "By law in Massachusetts, firewood is sold by the cubic foot. A cord contains 128 cubic feet." Then: "Many vendors advertise firewood by the cord, face cord, or truck load, or some other term. This is incorrect."

In practice, all 15 Wellesley-area sellers below name a cord or a fraction of one, and 7 of them also give cubic feet for at least one unit. If you buy in Massachusetts, the number to ask for and to see on the ticket is cubic feet.

### Minnesota: face cords are allowed, but they don't mean anything legally

Minnesota is the one state of the three that doesn't bar the term. Its statute, [Section 239.33](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/239.33), says a cord "shall mean 128 cubic feet of wood, bark, and air, if cut in four-foot lengths." For "sawed wood" a cord is "110 cubic feet when ranked, or 160 cubic feet when thrown irregularly or loosely," and for "sawed and split wood" it is "120 cubic feet, when ranked, and 175 cubic feet when thrown irregularly and loosely." Firewood cut to stove length and split fits that last description, so by the statute's numbers a stacked Minnesota cord of it can be 120 cubic feet, not 128.

The state Commerce Department's [firewood page](https://mn.gov/commerce/business/retailers/firewood/) says "You and the seller may enter into any agreement you choose, but be aware that words like truckload, face cord, rick, fireplace cord, or pile, have no legal definition," and that its Weights and Measures Division can't help in a dispute if the wood isn't sold by the cubic foot, the cubic meter or the cord. To count cords in a stack, it says to multiply height by length by stick length and divide by 120 for sawed and split wood. [Section 239.092](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/239.092) requires a delivery ticket showing "the quantity delivered" when the buyer and seller aren't both there to see it measured.

That fits what Minnesota sellers do: 8 of the 12 around Wayzata and Orono sell a "face cord" or "fireplace cord."

## Stacked vs loose: why a 180-cubic-foot "cord" can still be a cord

Wood thrown loosely into a truck or a pile takes up more room than the same wood stacked, because of the air between pieces. Minnesota's statute puts numbers on it: split wood that stacks to 120 cubic feet takes 175 cubic feet loose, so stacking shrinks it to about 69% of its loose volume. Two Massachusetts sellers sell loose cords with the same kind of math:

- **West End Firewood** sells "Loose Thrown Cord-Wood (180 cu ft)" and [explains](https://www.westendfirewood.com/product/cord-wood) that "A loose thrown cord should take up about 180 cubic feet," which when stacked "would equal roughly 128 cubic feet."
- **MetroWest Firewood** lists its [cord](https://metrowestfirewood.com/firewood) as "185cf loosely loaded (not stacked)," "Approximately a full cord," its half cord as 90 cubic feet loose and its quarter cord as 50.

At Minnesota's ratio, 180 loose cubic feet stacks to about 123, and 185 to about 127. The catch is that you can't check a pile. BostonFirewood.com's own [price page](https://bostonfirewood.com/our-kiln-dried-firewood/firewood-price) says it: "Piled wood you can not measure; stacked wood you can easily measure." Hopkinton's sealer warns that wood is often just dumped at the buyer's house, which makes the delivered amount hard to know, and says to stack it as soon as you can and measure it. In Massachusetts the legal quantity is the closely stacked volume, so stack a loose delivery before you judge it.

## How to measure a firewood delivery

You can check any delivery, whatever it was called, with a tape measure.

1. **Stack it tight first.** Pieces parallel, touching, in straight rows, the way the national rule describes "ranked and well stowed." If the seller doesn't stack, stack it yourself before you burn any.
2. **Measure three things in feet:** the height of the stack (check a few spots and average), the length of the row, and the piece length (measure several pieces and average). 16 inches is 1.33 feet; 12 inches is 1 foot; 18 inches is 1.5 feet.
3. **Multiply height x length x piece length** to get cubic feet. For more than one row, add the rows.
4. **Divide by 128** to get cords. In Minnesota, the state's own method divides split wood by 120.

Three examples:

- One row, 4 feet high, 8 feet long, 16-inch pieces: 4 x 8 x 1.33 = **42.7 cubic feet**, a third of a cord. That's one face cord.
- Two rows of 16-inch pieces, 4 feet high, 12 feet long: 4 x 12 x 2.67 = **128 cubic feet**, one full cord.
- A "face cord" of 12-inch pieces, 4 feet high and 8 feet long: 4 x 8 x 1 = **32 cubic feet**, a quarter cord.

If it comes up short, call the seller before you burn any of it. California's [buyer page](https://www.cdfa.ca.gov/dms/programs/qc/firewood.html) says to contact your local weights and measures office if the seller won't make it right, and suggests a photo of the stacked wood. In Massachusetts, towns have a sealer of weights and measures, and Hopkinton's page says to contact its sealer "if there is a problem with the delivery." Keep the delivery ticket: in Massachusetts it has to show the cubic feet.

## Questions to ask a firewood seller by phone

1. **"How many cubic feet is that, stacked?"** Get a number, not "a full cord." In Massachusetts it's the unit the law requires.
2. **"Do you measure it stacked or loose?"** If loose, ask how many cubic feet loose. The two loose cords above were 180 and 185.
3. **"How long are the pieces?"** You need this to size a face cord: 12-inch pieces make a 32-cubic-foot face cord, 16-inch pieces a 42.7.
4. **For a face cord, rick or pallet: "How high and how long is the stack?"** Or the pallet's dimensions, and whether they include the pallet.
5. **"Is stacking included, and if not, what does it cost?"** Several sellers above don't stack or charge extra for it, and BostonFirewood.com's owner said "I don't have any ability to stack right now."
6. **"Will the delivery ticket show the quantity?"** Massachusetts requires cubic feet on it; the national rule requires the quantity delivered, and so does Minnesota's law when you're not there to watch it measured.
7. **"Seasoned or kiln-dried, and what's in stock right now?"** Both sellers that answered on Black Friday had only kiln-dried wood.

Ask one question at a time and wait for the number. Our caller didn't always, as the next section shows.

## Where our caller fell short

- **It asked three volume questions at once.** At Beech Hill, the cubic-feet question came bundled with "stacked or measured loose" and "how long are the pieces," and the call ended before any of them got an answer. At BostonFirewood.com, the same bundle got one answer, "a hundred and twenty," and the piece length took a separate question later.
- **It turned the key question into a yes or no.** After hearing "a hundred and twenty," it asked "Is it 128" and accepted "you'll get more than that" without asking for the actual number. A better follow-up: "If I stack a full cord, how many cubic feet should I measure?"
- **It said "Great" before hearing the answer.** At Beech Hill it asked whether seasoned wood was in stock and said "Great" before anyone replied, twice. The answer turned out to be no.
- **It never asked about smaller units or the ticket.** No call asked how big a half cord or face cord is, or whether the delivery ticket would show cubic feet.

## How we gathered this

The sellers come from our three town firewood guides, where we searched for every business that delivers to Truckee, Wellesley, or Wayzata and Orono, from many sources rather than one directory. That gave 48 businesses that deliver to those towns. Private sellers (Craigslist, Marketplace and individuals) aren't counted or named. On November 27, 2026 we re-checked each business's own pages for the units it sells. 34 name a unit and are in the tables above. Of the other 14, 11 name no unit or size online beyond the word "cord" (or we only found an old post), and 3 sites returned "too many requests" errors every time we tried to re-check them that day, so we left them out rather than quote an older copy.

The legal sources are the agencies' and legislatures' own pages, read the same day: NIST Handbook 130 (2026 edition), California's regulation and its Division of Measurement Standards page, the Massachusetts General Laws and a Massachusetts town sealer's page, and the Minnesota Statutes and the Minnesota Commerce Department. We didn't buy wood or measure any deliveries; every size here is what a seller's page or a person on the phone said. Pages change, so check the seller's current page before you order.
