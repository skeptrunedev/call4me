**The short answer:** to negotiate a hospital bill, start by asking for an **itemized bill** and the hospital's **financial assistance policy**, then ask the billing office three questions: is there a **self-pay or prompt-pay discount**, do I qualify for **financial assistance (charity care)**, and can I get an **interest-free payment plan**. Every nonprofit hospital has to have a written financial assistance policy and post it online, and in most cases it cannot sue you, garnish wages or report you to credit bureaus for at least 120 days after the first bill. On our calls, the discounts were real but the "negotiation" wasn't: Advocate quoted a **50% uninsured discount**, HCA Florida Northside a **20% prompt-pay discount**, and when we asked about settling a balance after insurance, Cleveland Clinic said "That's going to be the payment plan" and Advocate said "We don't do any settlement options."

We called three hospital systems on Monday, October 26, 2026, with [call4me](/) asking the questions:

- **Cleveland Clinic** (nonprofit academic medical center, Ohio and Florida): **866-621-6385**
- **Advocate Health** (large nonprofit system, Illinois hospitals): **847-795-2300**
- **HCA Florida Northside Hospital** (part of HCA Healthcare, a for-profit hospital company): **800-733-0483**

We had no account and no bill at any of them, and said so, so these are the general answers any caller would get. All three reached a live person. HCA was fastest at **2 minutes 41 seconds**, after an AI assistant answered first; Cleveland Clinic took **4:57** and Advocate **5:18**, mostly hold.

## How to negotiate a hospital bill, step by step

1. **Get the itemized bill.** Ask billing for an itemized statement, not the summary. It lists every charge, so you can spot duplicates and things you didn't get. Expect to verify your account first; on our calls it took 3 to 10 business days at Cleveland Clinic and 15 to 30 days by mail at HCA. If you have Medicare, federal law gives you the right to one: when you ask in writing, the provider must send it within 30 days.
2. **Check it against your insurer's explanation of benefits.** Cleveland Clinic's representative said this is where most confusion comes from: the bill reflects what your insurance decided. Compare dates, quantities and services, and dispute anything you didn't receive.
3. **Ask for financial assistance before you pay.** Nonprofit hospitals must have a written financial assistance policy, and each hospital sets its own income limits. None of the three representatives could quote a limit on the phone, so ask for the written policy and application. Cleveland Clinic's site puts its limit at 400% of the federal poverty guidelines for Ohio residents.
4. **Ask about a self-pay or prompt-pay discount, and the deadline.** The discounts were the most concrete thing we heard: 50% uninsured at Advocate, 20% prompt pay at HCA (offered by email in the first 72 hours), and 35% at Cleveland Clinic for paying in full before care, per its site. They come with conditions, like having no insurance or paying on the first bill.
5. **If you can't pay in full, ask for an interest-free payment plan.** All three offered one. Cleveland Clinic's example was $150 over six months; HCA said no interest or fees.
6. **Don't expect a lump-sum settlement from the hospital.** Two of the three said no outright. Your leverage is the itemized bill, the discount and financial assistance, not haggling.
7. **Get it in writing.** Write down the name of who you spoke with, the date, and what they agreed to, and ask for a letter or a note on your account.

## Your rights under federal law

These are the federal rules behind those answers. Each one links to the government source. The 501(r) rules apply to nonprofit hospitals; the rest apply to all hospitals.

### Nonprofit hospitals must offer financial assistance (IRS 501(r))

To keep their tax exemption, nonprofit hospitals face [IRS requirements](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/charitable-organizations/requirements-for-501c3-hospitals-under-the-affordable-care-act-section-501r) for a written financial assistance policy, limits on what they charge eligible patients, and rules on billing and collections. Failing them can cost a hospital its 501(c)(3) status.

- **The policy has to be easy to get.** The [financial assistance policy rules](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/financial-assistance-policy-and-emergency-medical-care-policy-section-501r4) say the hospital must post the policy, the application and a plain language summary on its website (no account needed), mail paper copies free on request, and offer you the plain language summary at intake or discharge. The policy must state who is eligible, whether help means free or discounted care, how charges are calculated, and how to apply.
- **The IRS doesn't set the income limit.** Each hospital sets its own, which is why it pays to ask.
- **Eligible patients can't be charged full price.** Under the [limitation on charges](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/limitation-on-charges-section-501r5), if you qualify, emergency and other medically necessary care can cost no more than the "amounts generally billed" to people who have insurance, and other care must cost less than the hospital's gross charges.
- **You get time before collections.** Under the [billing and collections rules](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/billing-and-collections-section-501r6), the hospital must wait at least 120 days after the first bill after discharge before "extraordinary collection actions" like reporting you to credit bureaus, selling the debt, placing a lien, or garnishing wages. You have 240 days from that first bill to apply for help. Before any such action, it has to give you written notice at least 30 days ahead, along with the plain language summary.
- **Applying pauses collections.** If you send a complete application during those 240 days, the hospital must suspend collection actions, decide whether you qualify, and tell you in writing. If you qualify, it must refund what you paid above the amount you owe (unless that's under $5).

For-profit hospitals like HCA's aren't covered by 501(r), so their discounts and charity care come from their own policies and state law. HCA's representative still described an income-based financial assistance application, a separate uninsured discount, and a 20% prompt-pay discount.

### Uninsured or paying yourself: get a good faith estimate (No Surprises Act)

If you're uninsured, or not using insurance, providers must give you a written **good faith estimate** of expected charges. Under the federal rule ([45 CFR 149.610](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-B/part-149/subpart-G/section-149.610)):

- Care scheduled at least 3 business days ahead: the estimate is due within 1 business day of scheduling.
- Care scheduled at least 10 business days ahead: within 3 business days of scheduling.
- If you ask for one: within 3 business days of asking.

If your bill comes in **at least $400 more** than the estimate from that provider, you can dispute it with an independent reviewer ([45 CFR 149.620](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-B/part-149/subpart-G/section-149.620)). You have to start within **120 calendar days** of getting the first bill, and while the dispute is pending the provider can't send the bill to collections or charge late fees. The reviewer decides within 30 business days of getting the information. CMS's [page for people without insurance](https://www.cms.gov/medical-bill-rights/know-your-rights/no-insurance) explains how to start one. People on Medicare or Medicaid don't get good faith estimates under this rule.

### Insured: surprise out-of-network bills are banned in most cases

Since January 1, 2022, the [No Surprises Act](https://www.cms.gov/medical-bill-rights/know-your-rights) protects you from most surprise out-of-network bills for emergency care, air ambulances, and out-of-network providers (like anesthesiologists or radiologists) at an in-network hospital or surgery center. You [can't be charged more than in-network cost-sharing](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/no-surprises-understand-your-rights-against-surprise-medical-bills) for those services unless you got notice and agreed in writing. Ground ambulances generally aren't covered. If you think a bill breaks these rules, call the No Surprises Help Desk at **1-800-985-3059**.

### Hospitals must post their prices

Under [hospital price transparency](https://www.cms.gov/priorities/key-initiatives/hospital-price-transparency) rules, in effect since January 1, 2021, every hospital must post a machine-readable file with its standard charges, including its gross charges and its **discounted cash price** ([45 CFR 180.50](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-E/part-180/subpart-B/section-180.50)). It must also show prices for at least 300 "shoppable" services in a consumer-friendly way, or offer an online price estimator ([45 CFR 180.60](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-E/part-180/subpart-B/section-180.60)). The posted cash price is a useful number to bring to a negotiation. If a hospital hasn't posted it, you can complain to CMS.

## What to have ready before you call

Every representative wanted an account first: Cleveland Clinic asked for a medical record or account number, HCA for the name on the account, and Advocate asked whether you're a patient. Have these ready:

- The **account or guarantor number**, the name on the account and the dates of service from your statement
- Your **insurance card** and the **explanation of benefits** from your insurer, if you have one
- For financial assistance: **household size and annual income**, usually pay stubs or a tax return
- Pen and paper for names, dates and anything they agree to
