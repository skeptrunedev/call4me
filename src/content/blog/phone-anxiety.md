**The short answer:** phone anxiety is dread of making or answering phone calls: putting off the call, rehearsing it for an hour, letting it ring out, feeling shaky or sick before you dial. There's no separate diagnosis called phone anxiety. The NHS lists ["speaking on the phone"](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/social-anxiety/) among the everyday activities people with social anxiety worry about, and it is very common: in a 2025 [Research Co. poll](https://researchco.ca/2025/09/08/communication-na/) of 1,000 US adults, **50% said they feel anxious when they have to call someone they don't know**, and **57% of 18 to 34 year olds** said so.

What actually helps, in order:

1. **Lower the stakes.** Often the fear is about the live, unscripted part, where you can't take back what you said. Write down the one thing you need from the call and two sentences to get it. Scripts for the common calls are [below](#copy-paste-call-scripts).
2. **Practice the calls that matter to you, small and often.** The standard method for anxiety about social situations is gradual exposure: start with the easiest call, repeat it until it gets boring, then move up. This works better than waiting to feel ready.
3. **Hand off the errands you don't need to get better at.** Waiting on hold for your internet company isn't a skill you need to build. Have someone else make it, including an AI.
4. **Talk to a professional if it's running your life.** If you're missing appointments, losing work or avoiding people because of calls, see a doctor or therapist. The NHS calls cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) the best treatment, and the US National Institute of Mental Health calls it the "gold standard."

We make [call4me](/), an AI that makes phone calls for you, so we have an obvious interest in point 3. That's also why this page tries hard to be clear about where delegating helps and where it just keeps the fear going. call4me is not therapy or a treatment for anything.

Two things stand out. Feeling anxious about calls is far more common than the clinical condition. Both of those are US adults, though the surveys are two decades apart, so it's only a rough comparison: 50% against 7.1% means most people who dread calls don't have social anxiety disorder. They just don't like calling strangers. And in both Research Co. polls, people 55 and over report it least.

## Why phone calls give you anxiety

Some of the dread is reasonable. A phone call to a business is often a bad experience. We've timed 127 of our own calls to 74 customer service lines, and [38 of them never reached a person](/blog/how-long-to-reach-a-human) at all. The slowest that did took 25 minutes 9 seconds. Phone menus mishear you, holds don't say how long they'll be, and when someone finally answers, they ask for an account number you don't have in front of you. Not wanting to do that is a sensible reaction to a bad system, not a personal failing.

Unknown calls are a different, also reasonable, worry. In the Uswitch survey, 63% of the people who said they're likely to ignore a call said nuisance calls made them likely to avoid answering.

The part that's harder to justify is the fear of the conversation itself: being put on the spot, saying something stupid, not being able to see the other person's face, not being able to edit before you send. Here the research has good news. In a set of experiments published in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, Amit Kumar and Nicholas Epley asked people to think of someone they hadn't been in touch with for a while, often a friend they'd lost touch with, and get back in touch by phone or by email. People expected the call to feel more awkward. It didn't. "There wasn't any difference in how awkward they felt," Kumar said in [the University of Texas's write-up](https://news.mccombs.utexas.edu/research/when-we-fear-an-awkward-phone-call-we-often-turn-to-text/) of the [study](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000962), and the people who talked felt more connected. Their conclusion was that fear of awkwardness, not the actual experience, is what pushes people toward text.

That's the real shape of phone anxiety for a lot of people: the call itself goes better than expected, and the hour before it is miserable.

## Phone anxiety symptoms

Because phone anxiety is usually part of social anxiety, the NHS list of [social anxiety symptoms](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/social-anxiety/) is the most useful checklist. It says you may have social anxiety if you:

- worry about everyday activities, such as meeting strangers, starting conversations, speaking on the phone, working or shopping
- always worry about doing something you think is embarrassing, such as blushing, sweating or appearing incompetent
- find it difficult to do things when others are watching, and feel like you're being watched and judged all the time
- fear being criticised, avoid eye contact or have low self-esteem
- often have symptoms like feeling sick, sweating, trembling or a pounding heartbeat
- have panic attacks, where you have an overwhelming sense of fear and anxiety, usually only for a few minutes

With calls, this usually looks like: putting a call off for days or weeks, letting calls go to voicemail even from people you know, writing out every word beforehand, hanging up when someone answers, or replaying a short call in your head long after it ended.

The NHS also says that "someone with social anxiety feels overly worried before, during and after" social situations. With calls, before is often the worst part.

## How to get over phone anxiety

The standard approach, built into CBT for social anxiety, is to face the feared situation gradually and repeatedly, instead of avoiding it. NIMH describes exposure therapy as a CBT method "that focuses on progressively confronting the fears underlying an anxiety disorder to help a person engage in activities they have been avoiding."

[Anxiety Canada's guide to exposure](https://www.heretohelp.bc.ca/sites/default/files/facing-your-fears-exposure.pdf) names phone calls directly. For social fears, its example list includes "calling a friend on the phone," and for short situations it suggests "looping" them: "making consecutive phone calls until you feel more comfortable doing it." It also points out that you can make a step easier by changing how long it lasts: "talking to someone for 30 seconds is probably less scary than talking for five minutes."

Here is what that looks like as a ladder for calls. Rate each step 0 (no fear) to 10 (extreme fear) for yourself, and reorder it so it fits you. This one is an example, not a prescription:

1. Call a business that's closed and listen to its recorded hours. Hang up.
2. Call a store and ask what time it closes. One question, then "thanks, bye."
3. Do step 2 three times in a row at three different stores.
4. Call a restaurant and ask if they take reservations, then book one.
5. Leave a voicemail for a friend.
6. Call a friend with no reason except to catch up.
7. Call your doctor's office to book an appointment for yourself.
8. Make a call at work with a colleague nearby.
9. Answer a call from an unknown number.

Stay on a step until it feels dull, then move up. The anxiety drops with repetition, not with waiting. If a step is too much, make it smaller rather than skipping it.

### The catch with scripts

Scripts are standard phone anxiety advice, and they do help. But there's a catch that's easy to miss. In CBT for social anxiety, rehearsing exactly what you'll say can be a "safety behaviour": something you do to stop the feared outcome happening. The Centre for Clinical Interventions' [social anxiety workbook](https://www.cci.health.wa.gov.au/Resources/Looking-After-Yourself/Social-Anxiety) uses "Rehearse/plan what I'm about to say" as an example, paired with the belief "If I don't plan what I will say I will stumble over my words or say something stupid." It warns that safety behaviours "may help you feel more comfortable in the short term, but keep your anxiety going in the longer term," and that "It is not what you do, but why you are doing it that determines whether something is considered a safety behaviour."

So use scripts in two different ways:

- **For errands, a script is just good preparation.** Nobody calls their insurer without the policy number in front of them. Write it down and read from it.
- **For calls you're trying to get comfortable with, shrink the script over time.** Start with full sentences, then cut to bullet points, then to just the one thing you need. The goal is to find out that you can handle the call without it.

## Copy-paste call scripts

These cover four everyday calls: booking, rescheduling, asking a question and cancelling. Fill in the brackets, keep it in front of you, and read it word for word if you need to.

### Booking an appointment

> Hi, I'd like to book an appointment for [yourself / name], please. I'm [a new patient / an existing patient]. It's for [a checkup / the reason in a few words]. I'm free [days and times that work]. Do you have anything then?

Have ready: your date of birth, your insurance card (or say you're paying yourself), and your calendar. Before you hang up: "Can you repeat the day and time, so I can write it down?"

### Rescheduling an appointment

> Hi, I have an appointment on [day] at [time] under [name], and I need to move it. Could I come in [the days and times that work] instead?

If they offer something that doesn't work: "That one's hard for me. What's the next one after that?" Before you hang up: "So it's now [new day and time]. And my old one is cancelled?"

### Asking a question

> Hi, I just have a quick question. [Your one question, in one sentence]?

Then: "Great, thanks. One more: [your second question]." Write the questions down beforehand, in order, and tick them off as you go. If they don't know: "Is there someone else I could ask, or a better number to call?"

### Cancelling something

> Hi, I'd like to cancel my [service or subscription]. The account is under [name], and [the email or phone number on the account].

They may offer a discount to keep you. If you want to cancel, you can say the same sentence again: "Thanks, but I'd still like to cancel." Before you hang up: "Can you confirm it's cancelled, and that I won't be charged again? Will I get that in an email?"

### Lines for when you're put on the spot

- "I don't have that in front of me. Can I call you back with it?"
- "Sorry, could you say that again a bit slower? I'm writing it down."
- "Let me check and get back to you."
- "I'm not sure. What happens if I say no?"
- For voicemail: "Hi, this is [name], calling about [one-line reason]. Please call me back at [number]. That's [number] again. Thanks."

You're allowed to end any call to think. "Let me call you back" is a full sentence.

## Phone anxiety at work

Work calls can be harder because there are more of them, they're often unplanned, and colleagues can hear. A few things that make them easier:

- **Put calls on the calendar.** A scheduled call with an agenda is less of an ambush than a surprise one, and asking for a 15 minute slot is a normal request.
- **Send a short message first.** "Calling in five about the invoice" means both sides know what the call is for.
- **Follow up in writing.** A two-line email after the call ("as discussed, we'll ship Friday") removes the fear of having misheard or missed something.
- **Find a quiet room** for the calls you dread most, while you work up to making them at your desk.

If calls are a big part of your job and the anxiety is stopping you from doing it, that's exactly the kind of impact that makes it worth seeing a professional (more [below](#when-to-talk-to-a-professional)).

## Which calls to practice, and which to hand off

The Centre for Clinical Interventions lists "phone calls" and "attending appointments" among the situations people with social anxiety typically avoid, and avoiding them keeps the anxiety going. That's the core idea of exposure therapy, and it applies to handing calls to an AI as much as to letting them ring out. So it's worth being deliberate about which calls you give away.

**Worth practicing yourself:**

- **Calls with friends and family.** These are the calls where the relationship is the point. (call4me only calls businesses, so it can't make these for you anyway.)
- **Calls to your own doctor about your own health,** at least sometimes. You're the one who knows how you feel, and follow-up questions come up in the moment.
- **Job calls**: interviews, calls with your manager, calls with clients. These are skills that pay you back for years.

**Fine to hand off:**

- **Errands where the call is the obstacle, not the point**: getting through a phone menu, waiting on hold, asking a store if something is in stock, booking a haircut, moving an appointment, cancelling a subscription.
- **Calls that keep getting pushed back.** A done errand beats one you've been dreading for three weeks.

Handing off errands isn't only an AI thing. The mental health charity Mind's [guide to anxiety](https://www.mind.org.uk/media/7089/anxiety-and-panic-attacks-2021-pdf-version.pdf) notes that "you might not feel able to talk on the phone," and that "Some GP practices will allow someone else to ring up and book appointments for you (with your consent)."

## How to help someone with phone anxiety

If someone you care about avoids calls, Mind's advice for helping anyone with anxiety applies:

- **Don't push.** "Try not to put pressure on your friend or family member to do more than they feel comfortable with." Mind adds that being "forced into situations before they feel ready" can make anxiety worse.
- **Ask what would help.** "By asking them what they need or how you can help, you can support them to feel more in control themselves." Some people want you to make the call. Others want you to sit next to them while they do.
- **Help them get help,** if it's becoming a problem. Mind suggests offering to help arrange a doctor's appointment, or asking the practice about telephone appointments or home visits.

If you're helping someone who is anxious over the phone, the Samaritans' [listening tips](https://www.samaritans.org/how-we-can-help/if-youre-worried-about-someone-else/little-tips/) work on a call as well as in person: "Use open questions that need more than a yes/no answer," and "Pauses are fine, try not to jump in to fill a silence."

## When to talk to a professional

Phone anxiety on its own, the "I'll do it tomorrow" kind, is often something you can work through with the steps above. Talk to a doctor or therapist when:

- **It's having a big impact on your life.** That's the NHS's line: "It's a good idea to see a GP if you think you have social anxiety, especially if it's having a big impact on your life." In the UK you can also [refer yourself to NHS talking therapies](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/talking-therapies-medicine-treatments/talking-therapies-and-counselling/nhs-talking-therapies/) without seeing a GP first.
- **It doesn't go away and gets in the way of daily life.** NIMH's [guide to social anxiety disorder](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/social-anxiety-disorder-more-than-just-shyness) says the condition involves feelings that "last at least 6 months and interfere with daily life, such as work, school, or relationships," and suggests talking to a health care provider, "such as a primary care doctor, psychiatrist, psychologist, or clinical social worker."
- **It's about more than phones.** If you're also avoiding meetings, parties, shops or eating in front of people, it's worth getting a proper assessment.

The treatment is well established. NIMH calls CBT "the 'gold standard' choice for psychotherapy" for social anxiety disorder, and the NHS says "CBT is generally considered the best treatment." UK clinical guidance from [NICE](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg159/chapter/Recommendations) recommends CBT developed specifically for social anxiety disorder for adults. If making the call to book therapy is itself the hard part, that's a reasonable call to have someone else make.

If you're in crisis or thinking about harming yourself, in the US call or text **988**, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. In the UK, call Samaritans on **116 123**.
